1. Earth's Internal Structure

Earth is composed of distinct layers categorized by chemical composition and physical properties:

🏔️ Crust & Lithosphere

The rigid outer shell comprising the continental and oceanic crust along with the uppermost solid mantle. Broken into tectonic plates.

🌋 Asthenosphere & Mantle

The semi-fluid layer of hot rock directly beneath the lithosphere. Slow convection currents here drive tectonic plate movement.

🌊 Outer Core

A liquid iron and nickel layer whose movement generates Earth's magnetic field.

⚪ Inner Core

A dense, solid ball of iron and nickel under extreme pressure and high temperature.

2. Plate Boundaries & Interactions

Tectonic plates interact along three main types of boundaries, producing geological features like mountain ranges, ocean trenches, and earthquakes.

💥 Convergent Boundaries

Plates collide. Subduction occurs when dense oceanic crust sinks beneath lighter continental crust (forming trenches and volcanic arcs). Continental collisions form high mountain ranges.

🌋 Divergent Boundaries

Plates pull apart. Magma rises to fill the gap, creating new crust (e.g., Mid-Atlantic Ridge, continental rift valleys).

Transform Boundaries: Plates slide horizontally past each other. Friction builds up until stress is suddenly released as earthquakes (e.g., California's San Andreas Fault).

3. The Rock Cycle

Earth's crust is continuously recycled through geological processes over millions of years:

  • Igneous Rocks: Formed when molten rock (magma or lava) cools and crystallizes (e.g., basalt, granite).
  • Sedimentary Rocks: Formed when weathered rock particles are compacted and cemented in layers (e.g., sandstone, limestone).
  • Metamorphic Rocks: Formed when existing rocks are transformed by intense deep-earth heat and pressure (e.g., marble, slate).
